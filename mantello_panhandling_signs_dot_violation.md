# Troy Panhandling Signs: DOT Violation & Removal Order
## Research File — Story in Progress

*Last updated: July 15, 2026. Sources: WAMC (July 14), CBS6 (July 15 x2). Source interview: Council Member Greg Campbell-Cohen.*

---

## The Story in One Paragraph

Mayor Carmella Mantello installed approximately 12–20 anti-panhandling signs across Troy in 2025 without a city council vote. The signs — reading "It is okay to say no to panhandlers" and bearing a QR code to a nonprofit resource page — were placed at intersections across the city. One sign, at the intersection of Hoosick Street and State Route 7, was installed on a state DOT right-of-way. Democratic Council Member Greg Campbell-Cohen sent a letter to the New York State Department of Transportation citing federal MUTCD (Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices) standards; the state reviewed the complaint and determined the Hoosick/Route 7 sign violates DOT standards for color, location, and content. As of July 14, 2026, the city has been ordered to remove it within two weeks. Mantello's administration has not responded publicly to the order.

## The Signs

**Message:** "It is okay to say no to panhandlers"

**Secondary content:** QR code linking to a city webpage listing 40+ local nonprofits offering social services

**Quantity:** 12 signs. Confirmed by Mayor Mantello. (CBS6 had previously reported ~20; WAMC reported 12. Mantello's confirmation settles the discrepancy.)


**Locations confirmed:**
- River Street and Fulton Street (South Troy)
- Hoosick Street and State Route 7 (North Troy) — **the sign ordered removed**

**Authorization:** Authorized by Mayor Mantello's office. No city council vote appears to have been taken on the program. The initiative was framed as part of Mantello's "Safer and Cleaner Troy" agenda.

---

## The DOT Violation

**Standard cited:** The Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) — a federal document issued by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) that governs all traffic control signs on roads open to public travel, including state and local roads. Under federal law, the MUTCD applies to all jurisdictions that receive federal highway funds.

**Jurisdiction trigger:** Route 7 (Hoosick Street at that intersection) is a New York State highway. The right-of-way at that location falls under NYSDOT jurisdiction, not city jurisdiction. The other signs may be on city-controlled streets and therefore outside NYSDOT's reach.

**The complaint:** Council Member Greg Campbell-Cohen sent a letter to NYSDOT citing the federal MUTCD and raising three specific concerns about four of the twelve signs:
1. **Color** — The signs' colors do not conform to MUTCD standards for signage in that context
2. **Location** — The signs are placed at eye level for drivers at active traffic intersections
3. **Content** — Non-traffic messaging installed in a traffic-control environment

Campbell-Cohen's specific objection to the Hoosick/Route 7 sign: *"The actual traffic control signs are visually incidental to the garish anti-panhandling signs."* He argued that by drawing driver attention away from actual traffic control devices, the signs create a safety hazard.

**NYSDOT ruling (July 14, 2026):** The state determined:
- Only the sign at Hoosick Street and Route 7 falls under NYSDOT jurisdiction
- That sign violates federal MUTCD standards for color, location, and content
- The city must remove it **within two weeks**

**Administration response:** Mantello's office characterized the DOT order as a "recommendation" rather than a mandate. By the morning of July 15, one of the two signs previously on Hoosick Street had been moved to the 8th Street intersection, approximately 20 feet away. See full response section below.

---

## Administration Response: Move, Don't Remove

Faced with a two-week removal deadline, the Mantello Administration did not remove the sign. It moved it approximately 20 feet, from Hoosick Street (State Route 7, under NYSDOT jurisdiction) to the 8th Street intersection (under city jurisdiction). The sign's content, placement in a traffic environment, and visual competition with traffic control devices did not change. Only its address changed.

Mantello spoke to CBS6 on camera on July 15:

> "Long story short, we're here. It was moved about 20 feet. So, we're in compliance now and we have a great relationship with DOT."

> "DOT is all about safety also and also about walkable communities. We did move it for now, obviously, but there will be continuing dialogue."

Mantello also told CBS6 that a panhandler had previously been struck by a vehicle in the area. This claim has not been independently verified in available reporting.

The Administration plans to install additional signs in what it calls "identified problem areas" going forward.

**Key framing dispute:** The DOT engineer's email stated the city "must remove" the sign. The Mantello Administration characterized this as a "recommendation." The distinction matters: if the order was mandatory, the Administration did not comply with its spirit; it relocated the sign to avoid jurisdiction rather than addressing the underlying federal standards violation.

---

## Why It Matters Beyond the Sign

The MUTCD violation is not just a bureaucratic technicality. The federal manual exists to prevent non-traffic messaging from competing with safety signage at active intersections. Route 7 at Hoosick Street is one of the busiest corridors in the city. Mantello installed signage there without consulting state transportation authorities, without a council vote, and apparently without verifying whether the location was within city jurisdiction. The state had to step in.

**The broader pattern:** Mantello has a history of taking executive action with public infrastructure that later requires correction or reversal. This instance — unauthorized signage on a state right-of-way, ordered removed by federal mandate — adds to that record.

## Community Commentary: "We Don't Need Signs For That"

On September 25, 2025, writer Alissa Andersen published an essay in her Substack arguing that the signs are moral policing, not policy. Key arguments:

**The process failure:** The signs went up without City Council debate or a public hearing. Andersen contrasts Troy's process with Albany's: when Albany considered panhandling policy, more than 60 residents attended City Council hearings. Troy got twelve signs and no public input.

**The actual message:** Andersen argues the signs are designed to reassure comfortable residents they need not feel guilty about ignoring struggling neighbors. The city redirected charitable impulses toward nonprofits, but the primary message of the signs, as she reads them, is permission to look away.

**The First Amendment context:** Panhandling is protected speech under the First Amendment. The 1993 federal case *Loper v. New York City Police Department* established this. Cities that have taken harder legal stances against panhandling have faced successful challenges. Andersen cites Cleveland as a city that repealed a similar ordinance following litigation.

**The giving question:** Andersen argues that the decision to give directly to someone asking for help is a personal one between neighbors. Mantello's framing, that direct cash donations enable drug use and addiction, is contested. Signs and fines, Andersen writes, help no one with addiction issues.

## Nonprofit Response: Signs Aren't Working

Two major Troy nonprofits were asked to weigh in on the effectiveness of the campaign:

**Joseph's House** (Executive Director Amy LaFountain):
- Serves 2,500 unhoused individuals annually
- **No measurable increase in donations or referrals from the signs**
- Philosophical about the attention: "If the signs have brought you to our website, thank you. If the signs have made you angry and you want to support us — thank you."

**YWCA Troy** (Development Coordinator Katie Gallucci):
- Over 300 women on the waitlist for long-term supportive housing
- Critical of the sign design: *"The ways to contribute — it's very tiny. The main message of that sign, when you look at it, is 'don't do anything.'"*
- Called for messaging that reflects empathy and encourages all forms of giving

The city's stated rationale for the signs was to redirect charitable impulses toward established nonprofits. Neither of the two featured nonprofits reported that the signs had achieved that goal.

## The Vandalism Episode (June 21–22, 2026)

Overnight on June 21, 2026, unknown individuals placed decals over the anti-panhandling signs throughout Troy. The decals read: **"It is okay to care for your neighbors."**

**Mantello's response:**
> "It's childish, unproductive, and not to mention — illegal to deface public property. Our hope is that similarly to the individual who vandalized city hall and private businesses with spray paint — the perpetrator(s) are found and prosecuted to the limit of the law."

The mayor's office said the decals "did not come off easy. But they are off now." Troy Police Department launched an investigation. No arrests reported.

Council President Steele acknowledged the incident reflected broader concerns about public engagement with city initiatives.

## The MUTCD — What It Is and Why It Governs Here

The **Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices** is published by the Federal Highway Administration. It sets binding standards for the design, placement, and content of all traffic control devices on public roads in the United States — including state and local roads that receive any federal funding.

Key MUTCD principles relevant to this case:
- Non-traffic messaging (public announcements, advertising, civic campaigns) is not permitted in highway rights-of-way
- Signs must not create visual competition with actual traffic control devices
- Sign color, size, and placement must conform to specific standards by road type and speed limit

When a municipality installs a non-conforming sign on a state-controlled right-of-way, the state DOT has authority to order its removal. That is exactly what happened here.

The remaining 11+ signs on city-controlled streets are outside NYSDOT jurisdiction — but they may still be subject to separate city code requirements and, if on federally funded city roads, to federal standards as well.

## Key Open Questions for Further Reporting

1. **Did anyone in the administration check DOT jurisdiction before installing the Hoosick/Route 7 sign?** If not, this reflects a failure of basic due diligence. If they did and proceeded anyway, it's something else.

2. **What happens to the other ~11 signs?** NYSDOT said only the Hoosick/Route 7 sign falls under its jurisdiction — but that does not mean the others are compliant. A legal review of city sign code and MUTCD applicability on federally funded city roads has not been reported.

3. **Was there a council vote on the program?** Available reporting does not indicate council authorization. If the administration spent $500 in public funds on a public messaging campaign without council approval, the threshold is low but the principle matters.

4. **Will the city comply with the two-week removal deadline?** No administration statement confirms compliance. If the sign is not removed, the city risks further DOT action.

5. **Did the sign program achieve its stated goal?** Neither Joseph's House nor YWCA Troy reported a measurable increase in donations. No data from the city has been published showing a reduction in panhandling attributable to the signs.
