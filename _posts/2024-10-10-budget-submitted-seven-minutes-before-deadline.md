Mantello filed the 2025 budget seven minutes before the legal deadline. Corrected materials arrived almost an hour after it. Council President Steele, along with Council Members Vera and Spain-McLaren, said the timing "falls far short of the level of transparency that taxpayers deserve." On December 2, the Republican majority passed the budget 4-3. Steele and Spain-McLaren voted no; the third dissenting vote is not named in the clerk's minutes.

The city had no permanent comptroller from July 2024 through the entire budget season, leaning on outside firms that had already cost more than $260,000.

**The accounting crisis behind the budget**

At the October 15, 2024 special budget meeting, representatives from BST, the accounting firm the city brought in to reconstruct its finances, explained what it took to get the books into workable condition. The problem started with KVS, the city's financial software, which BST described as so outdated that accounting professionals compare it to Windows 95. The system runs as two separate systems: one where transactions are entered and a second where those transactions must be manually processed. When that processing runs, all users must log out and transactions must hold. Because the process is manual and sequential, entries regularly landed in wrong accounts or wrong funds. The prior comptroller had managed this by maintaining clearing accounts and doing reclassifications manually. When he left, no one else knew how.

BST found more than 60 journal entries it had to develop itself as part of the 2023 reconciliation, some involving multi-million dollar adjustments. A prior firm, ProNexus, had been brought in before BST to attempt to close the 2023 books; its work was later found unreliable by the state. The council had already appropriated ARPA funds for a new accounting software system. Those funds went unspent. At the October 15 meeting, Mantello said the administration was now moving forward on the upgrade. Implementation would take 12 to 18 months.

Mantello called delivering the budget at all a "herculean effort." Three comptroller candidates were in the pipeline at the time of the October presentation.

**The numbers**

The 2025 proposed budget was approximately $5 million larger than 2024, with every dollar of the increase coming from fixed mandated costs: pensions, health insurance, and debt service, which rose $1.1 million alone. The administration held the tax rate increase to 1.89 percent and came in $89,415 under the tax cap.

The garbage fee increased from $162, set as a temporary fee in 2018, to $252. The mayor called the increase necessary to compete with private haulers and acknowledged it was now a permanent fee. The budget also proposed a 10-cent-per-1,000-gallon water rate increase, approximately $1.46 per quarter for a four-person household.

The assessments director flagged a structural problem the budget did not address: the city's equalization rate had fallen to 65 percent. The last citywide property assessment was in 2011. The equalization rate is the state's measure of how far assessed values have drifted from actual market prices, and with years of a hot real estate market, the gap had widened significantly. A request for $10,000 to begin an assessment review was removed from the budget before submission.

**The delivery**

The budget arrived electronically near midnight on October 15. The document was not searchable and had no index. A hard copy did not arrive until the afternoon of the October 15 meeting. Steele put her frustration on the record: "I know for a fact that if you were sitting in this seat you would be just as frustrated as I am." Mantello defended the timeline, calling the circumstances extraordinary.
